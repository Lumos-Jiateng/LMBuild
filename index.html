LMBuild: Evaluating LLM Agents for Generating Buildable and Functional Structures

Can an LLM agent build an object that does more than look right: one that is functional as intended and buildable in the real world?

We introduce LMBuild, a benchmark for evaluating LLM agents on generating buildable and functional structures. Given a text prompt and an image, an agent uses tools to retrieve, create and place parts, then declares the joints, materials and assembly sequence that turn its geometry into something that could be fabricated and operated.

30 systems evaluated 200 core tasks 2,549 full-set tasks 12 metrics, 4 levels

FindingsKey takeaways

  • 1

    Looking right is no longer the bottleneck; working right is. Frontier closed models score in the high 80s on structural soundness and around 70 on design. On joints and kinematics (A.3) the best system reaches 42.7, and on simulated operability (R.3) none passes 22.1.

  • 2

    Strong models create, weaker models retrieve. Frontier agents gain from authoring their own parts. Weaker models lose ground once creation is allowed: Qwen3-VL-32B drops from 61.7 with retrieval only to 34.1 with creation only.

  • 3

    Specifying beats revising. Two extra revision rounds add about 2 points. Spelling out functional requirements adds +10.3 on functional parts and +12.0 on operability.

  • 4

    Open models have a part-creation gap. Closed models succeed on 95.7% of part-creation calls, open models on 51.7%. Most failures break CAD-kernel conventions or the program structure, not the geometry. Qwen3.5-27B (89.4%) is the exception.

  • 5

    Open models lose track of the scene. They refer to parts that were never placed 7.2 times per 100 calls, against 0.3 for closed models.

ResultsLeaderboard

How the scores are computed
  • ScaleEvery metric is on a common 0–100 scale. Cell colour runs from blue (low) through grey to orange (high), as in Table 2 of the paper.
  • MeanThe unweighted mean of the 12 metrics, shown only for systems that produce all 12. It is a ranking aid on this page; the paper reports the 12 metrics separately.
  • N/AThe system cannot produce the required output, for example a generator with no joints, materials or assembly order.

AnalysisDiscussion

Functional affordance and physical operability are the largest gaps

Most systems build connected (S.1), collision-free (S.2) structures, and frontier models already produce reasonable decompositions, appealing designs and reference-aligned shapes (D.1–D.3). Even the strongest systems remain imperfect on functional geometry (A.1) and complete functional parts (A.2). Joints and kinematics (A.3) are hard for everyone: most open models score below 25.

These gaps carry into realization. Agents name plausible assembly orders (R.1) and materials (R.2), but simulated operability (R.3) stays low for every system.

Strong models create, weaker models retrieve

We compare three part-access protocols: retrieval only, retrieval plus creation (the baseline), and creation only. Weaker models degrade sharply when they must author every part. Even offering creation alongside retrieval can hurt them, because more freedom means more decisions.

Frontier models show the opposite trend. They choose to create when both tools are available and stay as strong, or get stronger, when retrieval is removed. For them, the catalog is the constraint.

Tell the agent what the object must do

Letting agents inspect error reports and revise for two more rounds gives steady but small gains, about 2 points overall. Adding attribute and functional descriptions grounded in Wikipedia helps far more where models are weakest: +10.3 on functional parts and +12.0 on operability (p < 0.001), plus better kinematics.

Agents are more reliable at turning stated functional constraints into structure than at inferring them from an object name alone.

FAQ

What is LMBuild?

LMBuild evaluates LLM agents on generating 3D structures that can be built and that work. A generated object is an assembled structure: a meaningful part decomposition, joints, materials and an assembly sequence. The framework has three pieces: an interactive building environment, a curated benchmark built from established CAD datasets and enriched with Wikipedia knowledge, and a four-level evaluation.

What does the agent get, and what must it produce?

In the main setting the agent gets an object name and an image, for example “According to image W1, build a desk.” Using tools, it inspects and selects catalog parts, creates new parts from code, and places and adjusts them. It then declares joints, a material for each part and an assembly order. Other conditions add attribute or functional descriptions, or drop the image.

What are the interaction protocols?
  • Retrieve + create (baseline): catalog parts and newly created parts.
  • Retrieve only: catalog parts only.
  • Create only: every part is authored by the agent.
How is a build scored?

Twelve metrics in four levels. Soundness: connectivity, collision and stability, by rule-based geometry checks. Affordance: functional geometry against the reference, plus Wikipedia-grounded part and kinematics checks. Design: decomposition, aesthetics and alignment, by human-calibrated VLM judges with rule-based parts. Realization: assembly sequence, material choice and simulated operability.

Which systems are evaluated?

Six frontier closed-source APIs, thirteen open-source (multimodal) LLMs, and eleven domain-specific generators: brick models (BrickGPT, LegoACE), part-aware 3D generators (PartCrafter, PartPacker, Cube3D with CubePart), the same generators with Particulate for joints, and PhysX-Anything.

Where do the tasks come from?

LMBuild repurposes BrickNet, BrickComposer, PartNeXt, the Fusion 360 Gallery (Joinable), Artiverse and open-source hardware CAD, and grounds each object in verified Wikipedia and Wikidata knowledge. LMBuild-Core has 200 tasks and LMBuild-Full has 2,549. The Dataset tab lists them.
